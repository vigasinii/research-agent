# Black Holes

A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light or other electromagnetic waves, has enough speed to escape it. The boundary of no return is called the event horizon. Black holes form when massive stars collapse at the end of their lives, though they can also form through other mechanisms including the merger of neutron stars.

## Types of Black Holes

Stellar-mass black holes form from the collapse of stars more than about 20 times the mass of the Sun. They typically range from about 5 to several tens of solar masses. Cygnus X-1, discovered in 1964, was the first strong black hole candidate identified from X-ray emissions produced as it accreted material from a companion star.

Supermassive black holes reside at the centers of most large galaxies and range from millions to billions of solar masses. The Milky Way's central black hole, called Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*), has a mass of approximately 4 million solar masses. Intermediate-mass black holes with masses between stellar and supermassive are also known to exist.

## Direct Imaging

The Event Horizon Telescope (EHT), a global network of radio telescopes operating as a single Earth-sized instrument, captured the first image of a black hole shadow on April 10, 2019. The image showed the supermassive black hole at the center of galaxy M87, which has a mass of 6.5 billion solar masses and is located 55 million light-years from Earth.

On May 12, 2022, the EHT collaboration released the first image of Sagittarius A*, the black hole at the center of the Milky Way. Despite being much closer than M87, imaging Sgr A* was more challenging because material orbiting it moves much faster, requiring special image reconstruction techniques.

## Gravitational Waves

When two black holes merge, they produce ripples in spacetime called gravitational waves. The Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory (LIGO) made the first direct detection of gravitational waves on September 14, 2015, from the merger of two black holes 1.3 billion light-years away. This detection confirmed a major prediction of Einstein's general theory of relativity. The discoverers were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2017.

## Hawking Radiation

Stephen Hawking proposed in 1974 that black holes are not entirely black. Quantum effects near the event horizon cause black holes to emit thermal radiation, now called Hawking radiation, and slowly lose mass over time. For stellar-mass black holes, Hawking radiation is negligible on any practical timescale. Only microscopic black holes would evaporate quickly enough to be observationally significant. Hawking radiation has not yet been directly detected.
