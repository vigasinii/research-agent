# Exoplanets and the Search for Life

An exoplanet is any planet outside our solar system. The first confirmed detection of an exoplanet orbiting a main-sequence star was 51 Pegasi b, announced in 1995 by Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz, work for which they received the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics.

## Detection Methods

The transit method detects planets by measuring the dimming of a star's light as a planet passes in front of it. NASA's Kepler Space Telescope, launched in 2009, discovered over 2,600 confirmed exoplanets using this method. The Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS), launched in 2018, continues this survey with broader sky coverage.

The radial velocity method detects the gravitational wobble a planet induces in its host star. As a planet orbits, it pulls the star toward and away from Earth, shifting its light spectrum through the Doppler effect. This method is most sensitive to massive planets in short orbits.

Direct imaging captures actual light from exoplanets. This is extremely challenging because planets are dim compared to their host stars. The technique works best for young, massive planets far from their stars, which are still warm from formation and radiate infrared light.

## Habitable Zones

The habitable zone, sometimes called the Goldilocks zone, is the range of orbital distances from a star where liquid water could exist on a planet's surface. The boundaries depend on the star's luminosity and spectral type. Earth orbits within the Sun's habitable zone.

Proxima Centauri b orbits within the habitable zone of Proxima Centauri, our nearest stellar neighbor at 4.24 light-years. However, Proxima Centauri is a red dwarf that emits intense ultraviolet flares, which may strip away a planet's atmosphere.

The TRAPPIST-1 system, located 39 light-years away, contains seven Earth-sized planets, three of which orbit within the habitable zone. TRAPPIST-1 is a red dwarf star, and its planets are tidally locked, meaning one side always faces the star.

## Biosignatures

Scientists search for biosignatures, chemical signs of life, in exoplanet atmospheres. Oxygen is considered a strong biosignature because it is rapidly consumed by geological processes and must be continuously replenished, most plausibly by photosynthesis. Methane combined with oxygen is considered an especially strong indicator because the two gases react with each other and cannot coexist in large quantities without a continuous source.

The James Webb Space Telescope, launched December 25, 2021, has the infrared sensitivity to detect atmospheric biosignatures in some nearby exoplanet atmospheres, though definitive detection of life remains a future goal.
